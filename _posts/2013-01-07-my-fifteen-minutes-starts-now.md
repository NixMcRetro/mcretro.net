Without further delay, hurry over to [ChimeraRevo](https://web.archive.org/web/20190821224017/https://www.chimerarevo.com/guide/giochi/gaming-unallegra-chiaccherata-con-un-amico-113635/) to enjoy my first appearance there, and hopefully not the last! My good YouTube friend MasterControl90 invited me along for a chat about retro computers, video games and just about everything else that makes us want to spend our spare time surrounded by old hardware.

We ended up covering quite a lot of ground, from the early days of console gaming through to modern PC games and handhelds. The original conversation took place in English, but it was translated into Italian for ChimeraRevo. For anyone who doesn't speak Italian, [Google Translate](https://translate.google.com/) may be useful, although I'm not entirely sure what it'll do to some of my jokes!

### Retro Computers, Sega and Too Much Sunshine

The interview started with the rather personal question of who I am and what I enjoy doing. Well, I live and work in Sydney, Australia, which is a lovely place apart from one rather serious problem for a computer geek: far too much sunshine!

My main interests are retro computing and retro gaming, particularly repairing and modifying older machines. I like squeezing as much potential as possible out of hardware, whether that means improving its video output, changing a console from 50 Hz to 60 Hz or simply getting something working the way I think it should have worked when it was released.

We started our journey through gaming history with the 1970s and 1980s. Those years were a little before my time, but they certainly helped shape everything that followed. I even gave Nintendo credit for helping revive the video game market after the trouble surrounding the Atari era. And yes, E.T. deserves some of the criticism it gets. What a terrible game!

I'm much more of a Sega fan than a Nintendo fan, but I can still appreciate what Nintendo achieved with the NES. There was something about that console that made it particularly special, even compared with Sega's excellent Master System.

From what I remember of growing up in Australia, the Master System was extremely popular. I also remember visiting a friend who had one and being rather disappointed because I'd only experienced the Mega Drive before then. I hadn't quite worked out the difference between the two systems. Apparently I was expecting a little more from the older machine!

The discussion also wandered into early IBM PCs and their clones. By the late 1980s those machines were becoming increasingly capable gaming systems, gradually closing the gap with the home computers and consoles around them. It's easy to overlook those early DOS machines when talking about gaming history, but they were an important part of how we got here.

### My First 486DX and the Wonderful Horror of FMV

Then we moved into the 1990s, when computer hardware seemed to be advancing at a ridiculous pace. Commodore's decline was a particularly unfortunate part of that story, although there was certainly no shortage of interesting hardware appearing elsewhere.

Around the middle of the decade I got my first PC, a 486DX running at 66 MHz with a double-speed CD-ROM drive. I've since managed to track down and recreate that old machine as closely as possible, which should surprise absolutely nobody who knows what I'm like with these things.

That CD-ROM drive opened up a whole new world of games. Suddenly I could play Rebel Assault and Sam & Max, which is still a fantastic game. Unfortunately, it also opened the door to full-motion video. What had I done? When would this madness end?!

Which brings me to Road Avenger on the Sega Mega-CD. Have you ever played that thing? There was a truck that seemed determined to get in my way, and I could never work out how I was supposed to avoid it. I still have fond memories of the era, but some of those FMV games were a rather questionable idea. The cinematic sequences were brilliant, though!

We also talked about the move towards polygonal graphics. Chips such as Nintendo's Super FX and Sega's Virtua Processor helped push 3D graphics towards becoming the next big thing. It was an exciting period because you could see developers trying all sorts of new techniques, even if the results weren't always particularly elegant.

And while we're talking about interesting computers from that period, the Apple Macintosh LC 475 deserves a mention. It arrived around the same era as my 486DX and remains one of my favourite machines. There was plenty of interesting hardware about if you knew where to look.

### Red Alert, Half-Life and the Joys of Steam

Naturally, we couldn't discuss computer gaming without getting onto the subject of what happened to PC games over the years. MasterControl90 had plenty to say about buggy releases, downloadable content, disappointing console services and the way the industry had changed. We also touched on the modding communities and development tools that helped make older PC gaming so much fun.

For me, PC gaming really took off in the late 1990s with Westwood's Red Alert. The map editor was wonderful. You could fill a map with ridiculous numbers of enemies and then have enormous fun wiping them out. Sometimes the tools supplied with a game were almost as entertaining as the game itself.

Half-Life was another favourite. It's one of those games I can return to again and again without getting tired of it. But speaking of Half-Life, where on earth is Episode 3? What happened to it?!

MasterControl90 had some theories about a possible Half-Life 3 and Valve's future hardware plans, but I was still wondering what had happened to the episode we were supposed to be getting. So much waiting!

Steam, on the other hand, had already proved itself extremely useful to me. I'd lent my Half-Life 2 discs to a friend and never got them back. Whether they were lost, sold or simply vanished into another dimension, I couldn't tell you. Fortunately, the game was registered to my Steam account, so I could download it again whenever and wherever I wanted.

I was also excited about Steam expanding beyond Windows to Mac OS and Linux. With those systems increasingly sharing x86 processor architecture, the possibilities for running the same games across different platforms were rather interesting. It's the sort of development I would have loved to see much earlier.

### Game Boys, Angry Birds and My Trusty Nokia

Our final topic was portable gaming, which brought back some particularly good memories. We discussed Nintendo's Game Boy, the later handheld consoles from Nintendo and Sony, and the growing number of games being released for smartphones and tablets.

Unfortunately, mobile gaming also gave me an excellent excuse to complain about Angry Birds. First there was Angry Birds, then Seasons, Rio, Space and Star Wars. Can you see where I'm going with this? Sometimes a successful idea becomes a franchise that just keeps growing until the whole thing starts to feel a little excessive.

Give me Tetris on the original Game Boy any day. I have wonderful memories of Christmas holidays during the mid-1990s, staying on a farm in the Australian countryside outside Sydney. I'd fall asleep with my headphones on, listening to the Type B music from Tetris. Those were certainly different times.

If only I'd had rechargeable batteries back then! That would've made the whole experience considerably cheaper.

I have enjoyed some more recent handheld games, particularly the Golden Sun series. The first two games on the Game Boy Advance remain my favourites, though. As for the Nintendo 3DS, I tried it for a while and simply couldn't see the attraction. It wasn't really for me.

Smartphones weren't particularly appealing either. My phone of choice was a red Nokia 1112, and I was perfectly happy with it. The battery could last more than two weeks on a single charge, which was rather impressive.

Of course, perhaps that was because I could only play Snake II so many times before the keypad threatened to fall apart. Modern technology, eh?
