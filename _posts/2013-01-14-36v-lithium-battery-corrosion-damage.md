Here's a peek at what damage those little power cells can wreak on poor unsuspecting motherboards. It just happened to be an LC 475 as well, didn't it? Not mine thankfully! :P

This Macintosh has suffered some rather nasty damage from an old lithium battery. The corrosion has travelled a fair distance across the board, reaching the connectors and several surrounding components. Have a look at the state of those ports!

The particular battery in this machine was a red Maxell, which is a brand I've encountered in a few older computers. I've seen other types of batteries cause trouble too, but this is a particularly ugly example of what can happen when one leaks.

There are also a couple of electrolytic capacitors nearby that look rather suspicious. The 100 uF components in particular have some residue around their connections, while some of the other solder joints still look nice and shiny. As if one source of corrosion wasn't enough!

Interestingly, this LC 475 has a TDK power supply with the white switch at the rear. It's a slightly different arrangement from some of the other units I've been working on, but I'm much more interested in the damage across the motherboard today.

Apple specified a 3.6 V lithium PRAM battery for the LC 475. Old lithium batteries in stored vintage Macs are worth inspecting and removing where appropriate, as leakage can cause serious logic-board damage.

If you're putting a machine into long-term storage, it's worth checking the condition of its backup battery rather than simply leaving an ancient cell sitting on the motherboard. Handle and dispose of lithium cells carefully rather than treating them like ordinary rubbish.

This wasn't my Macintosh, thankfully, but it certainly makes me want to check every old computer I've got sitting around here. The damage from a single little battery can be extraordinary.
