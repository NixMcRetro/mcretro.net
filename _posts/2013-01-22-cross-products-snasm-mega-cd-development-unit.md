Here's the latest addition to the collection. It cost me a pretty penny, but it is certainly one of the highlights now. This is a Cross Products SNASM Mega-CD development unit.

It's finally arrived, and I've managed to have a quick play with it. The good news is that it appears to work! I thought I'd put it alongside an ordinary Mega Drive and Mega-CD combination to see what was different.

For starters, the development unit is a little taller than the retail hardware. You probably wouldn't notice until you put the two side by side, but the cases certainly aren't identical.

There are also a whole collection of switches and connectors that the ordinary Mega Drive doesn't have. At the moment I've set the DIP switches to off so the unit will boot, although I haven't fully worked out what all the different configurations are for.

Cross Products built the SNASM Mega-CD around modified Mega Drive and Mega-CD hardware, with additional development electronics allowing it to interface with a PC and development tools.

The system evolved into the SNASM2 environment, which is the PC-side hardware I eventually ended up using with this particular unit. It could also work with dedicated CD-emulation hardware, allowing development data to be supplied from the PC without continually producing physical discs.

One interesting thing about the casing is that the Mega Drive and Mega-CD sections appear to have been adapted from fairly ordinary retail hardware. There are familiar shapes and cut-outs in unusual places, including a volume-control opening that's no longer where you'd expect it.

The rear is considerably more interesting. Alongside the usual connectors, we've got SCSI and emulator connections for the development equipment. There's also a cooling fan on the side with some lovely red detailing. Everyone knows red bits make a machine faster!

This particular unit appears to have some connection with Code Monkeys, and it arrived with paperwork and several floppy disks. Some were labelled for Mega-CD and 32X development work, so those will need to be examined and archived.

I haven't received all the PC interface boards yet, which means I can't explore the full development setup at the moment. Still, I've already got plenty to investigate. The unit carries Cross Products markings from 1992, and the BIOS reports version 1.10. I also noticed some interesting identification labels and numbers, which might help trace its earlier life.

For now I'm just enjoying the fact that one is sitting here. The next jobs are to power it up properly, pull it apart, photograph everything, and work out exactly what survived inside. Looking forward to this one.
