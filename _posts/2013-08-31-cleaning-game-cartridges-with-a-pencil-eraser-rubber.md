"Throw out your isopropyl! Pencil erasers are here!" Well, perhaps not literally, but I thought I'd demonstrate another method I'd been experimenting with for cleaning old game cartridges.

The example here was a Super Nintendo cartridge, *Super Mario All-Stars*, with some rather dirty contacts. One side was looking considerably cleaner than the other, so it made a good before-and-after comparison.

I'd been using a Faber-Castell pencil eraser to rub away some of the stubborn grime on the cartridge's edge connector. The difference in appearance was quite impressive. The contacts went from looking rather dull and filthy to something almost golden again.

I don't know why we call these things rubbers in Australia. Apparently that word means something rather different elsewhere. We were so innocent when we were children!

The trick certainly seemed effective at removing the visible dirt, but there is a drawback worth understanding. Erasers are mildly abrasive, and repeatedly rubbing the contacts can wear away their thin metal plating. I'd treat this as something I experimented with at the time, rather than a recommendation to scrub every cartridge until the contacts shine.

A gentler cleaning method appropriate to the connector should be the starting point, especially with old or valuable games. Removing the dirt is one thing, but damaging the contacts in the process rather defeats the purpose.

I was also hoping this would put an end to the ancient practice of blowing into game cartridges. You know the one. The game won't start, so you blow into the bottom of the cartridge and hope for the best. That introduces moisture, which isn't particularly wonderful for the electrical contacts.

Unfortunately, I couldn't demonstrate the cleaned cartridge working in my Super Nintendo at the end of the recording. The console I'd been using had developed another fault, and was now refusing to produce a picture.

This was the same machine that had previously suffered from audio problems involving its S-MIX chip. That chip had a rather spectacular-looking hole in it, and now the console was giving me video trouble too. Excellent. From no sound to no picture!

So the recording showed the cleaning result, but not a successful before-and-after gameplay test. The contacts certainly looked better, although that alone doesn't establish that the cartridge had been repaired or that the technique was suitable for every connector.

The same sort of dirt can turn up on cartridges for the Mega Drive, NES, Master System and plenty of other consoles. Whatever system you're working on, it's worth thinking about the condition of both the cartridge and the slot before deciding which method to use.

And don't forget, a lovely clean cartridge isn't much use if the console's connector is packed full of twenty years of fluff and rubbish. I'll have another video on cleaning those slots as well.
