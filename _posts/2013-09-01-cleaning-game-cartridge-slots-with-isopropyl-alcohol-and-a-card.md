Ah, the trusty old Mega Drive 2! This particular one has already been modified with a couple of switches around the back, but today I thought I'd give its cartridge connector a clean. You'd be amazed how much rubbish can collect inside these things over twenty years.

I'd already been experimenting with ways to clean the contacts on game cartridges, but there's not much point cleaning the game if the slot inside the console is filthy. Insert the cartridge a few times and you can end up transferring some of that grime straight back onto the contacts.

For this experiment I was using isopropyl alcohol, specifically a bottle marked 99.8%. I preferred the high-purity stuff because it contains very little water compared with the more common 70% solutions. It also evaporates fairly quickly, although that doesn't mean it's a good idea to soak an entire game console in it.

The other pieces of equipment were rather less exciting. I had a plastic card and some cleaning material that could be folded around its edge. Nothing particularly elaborate, but it allowed me to reach down into the cartridge connector.

With the machine disconnected from power, I used the material around the card to clean between the contacts. The idea was to insert and remove it carefully rather than twist it from side to side, since the little spring contacts inside the slot aren't particularly enthusiastic about being bent out of shape.

The amount of dirt coming out was quite impressive. Every time I moved to a clean section of the material, another patch of grime appeared. Lovely! Just what you want to discover inside your favourite game console.

Of course, the material needs to be suitable for the connector, and it's important not to leave lint or debris behind. Too much pressure or an unsuitable card can damage the contacts, so this isn't something I'd suggest forcing into every cartridge slot you encounter.

The same goes for the cleaning liquid. Only a small amount is necessary, and the connector needs to be completely dry before the console is powered on again. There's no great advantage in swapping one electrical problem for another.

I'd also been having a play with my Sega Genesis Nomad around the same time. The little portable console had been giving me some trouble with cartridges, so it seemed like another candidate for a bit of cleaning and testing.

The video wandered off into a demonstration of the Nomad, which I still think is a rather wonderful machine. It's almost the size of a regular handheld console, provided you ignore the enormous battery pack hanging off the back!

I even considered taking it on the train. A portable Mega Drive sounds like a brilliant idea until you remember how much hardware and battery power it takes to make the thing run.

Anyway, back to cartridge slots. This approach was intended for the sorts of connectors found in systems like the Mega Drive, Super Nintendo and Famicom, not every cartridge connector ever produced.

And I wouldn't expect it to solve all the problems with the Sega Saturn's expansion cartridge slot. That thing seems determined to be troublesome no matter what you do to it!

It's also worth opening up old consoles occasionally to inspect the general condition of the boards, particularly for leaking capacitors, corrosion or evidence of liquid damage. I repair computers for a living, and believe me, people can do some remarkable things to electronic equipment.

So that's another cartridge connector cleaned. Now perhaps the games will behave themselves for a while, although knowing my collection, something else will probably break tomorrow!
