Well, we didn't cut it up for science... or did we? Poor Seagate, if they can't get 20GB right, what hope do we have for larger drives? Just kidding! I know all drives have potential to fail. It's just it always seems to be Seagate when I look at the label. Bad luck I suppose!

This particular 20 GB drive was already faulty, so I thought I'd take the lid off and have a look at what was hiding inside. Don't do this to a perfectly good hard drive! Taking one apart is a pretty effective way of making sure it won't work again.

I've never been particularly fond of Seagate's mechanical drives. I tend to prefer Western Digital for spinning hard drives and Samsung for SSDs. I wasn't especially thrilled when Samsung sold its hard-drive division to Seagate, either. That said, I've had my share of troublesome Western Digital Black drives too. Everything fails eventually!

After locating the obligatory hidden screw, we finally get a nice view of the platter, actuator arm and the magnets that help move the heads around. There's also a mechanism for parking the heads safely when the drive isn't being used. Quite a lot of clever engineering crammed into a little metal box.

The platter has a rather beautiful mirror finish, and you can even see the camera reflected in it. It looks like there's only one platter, with a head on one side. I even had a good look for the bad block, although I couldn't see anything particularly suspicious!

Of course, after taking the mechanism apart, this particular drive isn't going to be reading anything again. Not that it was doing a particularly good job of that beforehand!

And speaking of failing hard drives, make sure you've got backups! I've got two backups of all my data, and I'm also using RAID 1 with Western Digital Green drives for my essential storage, around 3 TB at the moment. Having that redundancy is handy if one of the drives fails.

The backup storage only gets powered on about once a month. Well, every two weeks. Okay, maybe once a week when I get around to backing everything up! You can never have too many backups.
