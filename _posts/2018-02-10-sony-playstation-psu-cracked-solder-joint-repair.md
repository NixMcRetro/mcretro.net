Nobody likes a gaming console that can't game. On this original Sony PlayStation, repeated movement at the figure-8 mains inlet had cracked the solder joints where the AC connector meets the power-supply PCB. Time for a little repair, although naturally there's a bit more going on with this machine than just a couple of suspicious-looking solder joints.

This is a 240 V power supply that I was preparing to use in an early SCPH-1001 PlayStation. That particular console normally came from a 110-120 V market, and I really don't enjoy using step-down transformers when I can avoid them. Of course, changing an internal power supply means finding one that is actually compatible with the console, not simply one that happens to fit inside the case.

I'd been looking at some other PlayStation revisions too, including the SCPH-5502. Unfortunately, not every PlayStation power-supply board uses the same connector arrangement. Some have different numbers of pins and different layouts, so it's important to check what you're dealing with rather than assuming every original PlayStation PSU can be swapped into every other model.

The cracked joints were on the underside of the board, right where the figure-8 mains connector was soldered in place. They weren't particularly obvious at first, but a closer inspection showed little cracks around the connections. That's the sort of fault that can lead to intermittent power or arcing when the connector moves.

I also noticed an area of dark discolouration around another component. That wasn't especially encouraging, and I made a note to investigate it another time. The board I was replacing had been considerably cleaner in that area, but it wasn't useful for this particular setup, so I was working with what I had.

For the damaged connector joints, I cleaned up the existing solder connections and applied fresh solder to restore them. Naturally, the Hakko decided to display a hardware error just as I was getting started. It's amazing how often the repair equipment itself chooses the worst possible moment to have a little tantrum!

Once the iron was behaving again, I finished the two connections. The joints looked substantially better, and there was no longer the obvious cracking that had been visible beforehand. That fixes the physical soldering problem I found, although it doesn't establish the condition of every other component on the board.

Similar mechanical solder failures can happen wherever connectors are repeatedly pushed and pulled, including on some other consoles. But the exact arrangements differ. The Mega Drive 2, for example, uses an external low-voltage adaptor rather than the internal mains-connected figure-8 socket on this PlayStation.

As this is mains-powered hardware, there's also a serious safety consideration. Stored charge can remain inside a PSU after disconnection, and working on the board requires proper equipment and appropriate training. This is a record of the repair I carried out, not an invitation to poke around a PlayStation power supply with a soldering iron.

At least the visibly cracked joints are now repaired. Another little job ticked off the list, and one more old console power supply looking slightly more respectable!
