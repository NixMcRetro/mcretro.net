Quick, down memory lane! This one was a fun video to put together, slowly piecing an AMD Athlon K7 onto a brand new, old stock motherboard. All-in-all it went together quite well. This PC went on to be used for testing the Sega Dreamcast HKT-0120 dev kit. It worked a charm too. Then it got passed on to some kids who ended up setting it on fire... Good times?

The motherboard is an MSI Slot A model, still sealed in its original box after all these years. I was rather excited to open it up and see what we'd have received at the turn of the millennium. There's plenty of wonderful packaging artwork, a Y2K approval claim and all the usual promises of astonishing computing performance.

One of the reasons I wanted a machine from this era was its mixture of expansion slots. We've got AGP for graphics, PCI for the more modern cards and ISA for older hardware that I still want to experiment with. That makes it rather useful for development equipment from several console generations, including my Sega hardware and assorted ISA cards.

I had an AMD Athlon processor ready to go, although I managed to confuse myself about its speed. The marking made me think I'd acquired a 750 MHz model, but on closer inspection it was actually a 500 MHz Athlon. So now I've got two 500 MHz processors. Apparently reading the label correctly is an optional part of computer building!

The graphics card is a 3dfx Voodoo5 5500, which makes this a rather entertaining late-1990s-style machine. I've also got a SCSI card for the Dreamcast development kit, a collection of SDRAM modules and a parallel ATA CD-ROM drive. It's not exactly what most people would choose for a modern workstation, but then I'm not building a modern workstation.

Everything went into a Corsair case, which looks rather fancy for hardware of this age. The ATX layout certainly makes it easier to fit the components, although there were a few missing cosmetic bits and plenty of cable-management opportunities. One advantage of a large case is that you can actually get your hands inside without removing half the machine first.

After assembling the computer, I gave it a quick test with Linux and had a look at whether the hardware was behaving. The Voodoo card appeared to be working, and the machine was happy enough to get through the initial tests. I still needed to decide exactly how I wanted to configure its long-term storage and operating system.

Windows 98, Windows 2000 and Windows XP were all possibilities. I'd always had a soft spot for Windows 2000 because it was considerably more stable than the older Windows 9x releases, at least in my experience. On the other hand, Windows 98 has a certain nostalgic appeal when you've got a Voodoo card and hardware from that era.

I eventually spent some time getting Windows 98 running, installing drivers and investigating the motherboard's chipset. The old MSI documentation was useful for identifying the AMD-751 and AMD-756 components, although finding archived BIOS files and drivers for obsolete motherboards can be a challenge in itself.

There was plenty of unrelated rambling during the build, including my upcoming university examinations. As usual, I was wondering why I hadn't done quite as much study during the semester as I'd planned. It turns out that taking apart computers is substantially more interesting than revising for exams. Who could have guessed?
