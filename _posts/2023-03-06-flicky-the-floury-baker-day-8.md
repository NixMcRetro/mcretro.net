There was an awful lot to read about the cicada life cycle, especially what happens after hatching. The little nymphs need to get to the soil and find roots. I hoped Flicky's eggs might eventually get that far.

We're now eight days into this unexpected adventure with Flicky the Floury Baker, and she's still spending time on the branches I've provided. One of those branches has already been used for egg laying, and I was rather interested in how she seemed to remember exactly where she'd been working.

During the night I'd moved her onto another part of the arrangement, only to find that she made her way back towards the same section of branch. Perhaps she was recognising something about the wood, or perhaps it was simply the most suitable place available. I can't really say, but it was fascinating to watch.

I took a closer look at the little cuts left in the branch by the ovipositor. They're surprisingly noticeable once you know what you're looking for, although my camera wasn't particularly interested in focusing on them. It's amazing how much damage a small insect can make when she's depositing eggs into wood.

The branches themselves are something I've been trying to manage rather carefully. They're no longer attached to a living tree, so there's only so much moisture and nutrition they can provide. I've had some of them sitting in water, but that isn't quite the same as allowing Flicky to feed from a healthy tree outdoors.

I was also concerned about her damaged wings and what might have happened if I'd left her outside where I found her. She may have struggled to fly or move between trees, and there are certainly plenty of ants and other predators or scavengers around. I can't know exactly what would have happened, but I wanted to give her somewhere quiet to rest.

There was more experimenting with fresh branches throughout the day. Sometimes Flicky appeared interested in feeding, while at other times she would walk around and investigate the available twigs. I spent a rather ridiculous amount of time trying to understand what she was doing. I've certainly learned more about cicadas in the past week than I ever expected to!

The other challenge is what to do if the eggs develop and hatch successfully. Cicada nymphs need to reach soil and find suitable roots after hatching, which means I can't simply leave the branches in an ordinary container indefinitely. I've been reading about their lifecycle and trying to work out a practical way to provide the right conditions.

There are still a lot of things I don't know. I can't confirm how many eggs are in any particular slit, and I don't want to damage the branches just to find out. For now, I'd rather keep them as healthy as possible and see whether anything develops naturally.

By the end of the day Flicky was still moving about and showing interest in the branches. We're not exactly running a professional entomology laboratory here, but I'm doing my best to look after her and whatever eggs might be tucked away in the wood.

Eight days already. Who would have thought one little cicada could keep me this busy?
