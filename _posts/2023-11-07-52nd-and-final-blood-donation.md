A few months ago I decided I was finished with donating blood. A few factors decided into why I would no longer donate.

1\. [The way our blood service ran in Australia](https://theconversation.com/how-australia-can-fix-the-market-for-plasma-and-save-millions-101609). Whole blood is all here and plasma is purchased from the overseas where donors are reimbursed for... well, see number two.

2\. Blood and tissue can only be donated freely in Australia - no reimbursement for iron pills, travel or even my time. Perhaps I am desensitised to that fuzzy warm feeling of cold saline hitting my vein when giving plasma.

3\. Pain. Recently my primary vein for donating has experienced pain. Whether that was a result of poor needlework or not I couldn't be sure. But I do know from past experience - the needle doesn't have to be completely inserted two inches deep. The only times I've had pain is when that happens.

![](/assets/images/2023/img_1191.jpg)

I reached the 50 donation milestone. I was vegan at the time and got a block of Cadbury Dairy Milk Chocolate Block. Too bad it wasn't a Whittaker's Oat Milk Chocolate Block. I had to decline and being socially anxious made me more uncomfortable than usual for the donation.

![](/assets/images/2023/img_1192.jpg)

And at the very end here we have the 52nd, and final donation! Whole blood was a good way to go out as whole blood is how it all started back in October 2014 in a mobile blood van. For now I'll roll my sleeves down and keep my iron stores self-contained. Plus I get to keep all those [PFAS chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per-_and_polyfluoroalkyl_substances) that I would usually lose through plasma.

Weirdly enough, there was some science hiding behind that joke. A 2022 Australian trial in firefighters with elevated PFAS found that regular blood or plasma donation reduced some serum PFAS concentrations, with plasma having the larger effect. That does **not** make donation a general PFAS treatment for everyone, but at least the joke had a paper behind it.

![](/assets/images/2023/img_1195.gif)

Here's the tip zone, or rather the bit where I need to stop pretending my personal donor routine is universal advice. A whole-blood donation removes roughly 250mg of iron. Haemoglobin screening can still be normal while iron stores are low, so frequent donors can run down ferritin without necessarily failing the pre-donation haemoglobin test. That does not mean everybody should automatically take iron supplements. Whether supplementation is appropriate depends on iron status, diet, other sources of blood loss, donation frequency and clinical advice.

The whole-blood arithmetic was broadly right. At about 250mg per donation, four whole-blood donations would remove about 1000mg of iron across the year. How quickly that is replaced varies from person to person.

I got the plasma maths badly wrong. Lifeblood lists approximate iron loss as about 10mg for a plasma donation and 48mg for a platelet donation, compared with about 250mg for whole blood. Twelve plasma donations would therefore be roughly 120mg of iron loss, not 750mg; 18 would be about 180mg, not 1100mg. The red cells being returned is why plasma donation has a much smaller effect on iron stores than whole blood.

![](/assets/images/2023/img_1193.jpg)

Alternate between plasma and whole blood? Whole blood is still the big iron hit. There's actually a good summary on the [Lifeblood website](https://www.lifeblood.com.au/health-professionals/clinical-practice/clinical-indications/iron-deficiency-anaemia/blood-donors). The [scientific paper I found](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/bjh.15136) was useful for understanding iron deficiency in blood donors, but I had applied the numbers too broadly.

Another thing I had been reading about was alternate-day iron. Studies in iron-depleted or iron-deficient women found that oral iron can raise hepcidin for about 24 hours and that fractional absorption may be higher with alternate-day dosing. That does not turn 100 to 200mg of elemental iron every other day into a universal donor prescription. Vitamin C can affect non-haem iron absorption, but saying it simply "overrides" hepcidin was too strong. Supplement dose and schedule should depend on the person's iron status, diet, tolerance and advice from a GP or pharmacist.

![](/assets/images/2023/img_1194.jpg)

And there we have it, the end of an era. 25 whole blood, 25 plasma, 2 platelets. So long and thanks for all the mini pretzels! 🥨 💁‍♀️
