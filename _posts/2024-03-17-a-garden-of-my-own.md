![Spreading grevillea shrub covered in pale yellow flowers among mulch, strappy green plants, and a lawn edge.](/assets/images/2024/img_1282.jpg)

*Grevillea juniperina 'Gold' spreading across the mulch. Pretty good argument for making room for native plants instead of yet more lawn.*
{: .media-caption }

It's been almost a month since [I last posted](/the-garden-lizards-and-bees/) about the garden. I guess we're due for a bit of a dive into what I would populate my garden with if I had a garden I owned, not rented.

![Close-up of pale yellow grevillea flowers with long curled styles among fine dark-green needle-like leaves.](/assets/images/2024/img_1284.jpg)

*A closer look at those spectacular curly grevillea flowers. I'm starting to think the bees would approve.*
{: .media-caption }

Let me be clear, there would be no buffalo grass. Weeds would probably roam free and it would be glorious. At that point are they even weeds?

![Low green grevillea shrubs with scattered small red flower clusters spreading across a mulched garden bed.](/assets/images/2024/img_1285.jpg)

*More grevillea ground cover. Imagine a whole stretch of this in place of the buffalo grass. Glorious!*
{: .media-caption }

But to help native creatures a little I'd probably install some sort of ground cover like spider flowers, [Grevilleas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grevillea).

![Dense green grevillea shrubs with many red flowers growing around an old rusted metal wheel in a mulched garden.](/assets/images/2024/img_1283.jpg)

*A sea of red grevillea flowers with an old rusty wheel thrown in for atmosphere. Far more interesting than a neatly shaved lawn!*
{: .media-caption }

So there'd be a bunch of native ground cover in the form of Grevillea juniperina. Handsome looking devils, aren't they?

![Hardware store shelves displaying wooden bee and insect hotels with drilled tubes and compartments, alongside packets of seeds.](/assets/images/2024/img_1267.jpg)

*The hardware shop's little collection of bee hotels was tempting. I'd still want to check the holes and materials before inviting anyone to move in.*
{: .media-caption }

I guess I'd probably hit up the local hardware store and buy a bunch of [bee hotels](https://entomologistlounge.wordpress.com/2017/09/18/insect-hotels-a-refuge-or-a-fad/), since my property was likely cleared by the previous owners. I'd need to check the nesting holes were smooth and the hotel could be kept dry and maintained, though. A cute little roof doesn't tell me much about that.

![Wide hardware store display of wooden bee and insect hotels, seed packets, and gardening products on orange metal shelves.](/assets/images/2024/img_1268.jpg)

*More bee hotels in the growing kits aisle. There were certainly plenty of options, but I'd want something the local native bees could actually use.*
{: .media-caption }

I have no idea where to find untreated wood so these would absolutely tide me, and the bees, over for a while.

![Potted lavender plants with purple flower spikes clustered around a white nursery sign labelled Lavender.](/assets/images/2024/img_1270.jpg)

*Lavender looked very tempting as a food stop for the blue-banded bees. Purple flowers everywhere, and plenty to choose from.*
{: .media-caption }

[Blue-banded bees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amegilla). Purple and blue flowers are a good place to start, though they aren't the only colours these bees visit. Let's load up with [lavender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavandula). It will grow above the ground cover quite well, and some varieties flower for a long stretch... I think.

![Close-up of potted lavender with narrow grey-green leaves, purple flower spikes, and some dried flower heads at a nursery.](/assets/images/2024/img_1269.jpg)

*A closer look at the lavender. Some flowers were already looking rather scruffy, but it was easy to see why I kept coming back to the purple ones.*
{: .media-caption }

Lavender isn't an Australian native, though. Maybe I should get some native daisies from [Brachyscome spp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brachyscome) instead.

![Long nursery bench packed with potted purple and pale pink flowering plants beneath Lavender signs and hanging flower baskets.](/assets/images/2024/img_1271.jpg)

*This enormous lavender display wasn't helping me make any decisions. How am I supposed to choose just a few plants from all that?*
{: .media-caption }

I guess there's a fair bit, really not sure what I'd go for.

![Potted Grevillea 'Little Red' shrub on nursery shelving, with slender green leaves and a plant label showing its red flowers.](/assets/images/2024/img_1272.jpg)

*Back to grevilleas! The 'Little Red' label promised a lovely low-growing one, which is exactly the sort of ground cover I had in mind.*
{: .media-caption }

Which brings us back around to grevilleas.

![Hand pointing to the rear care label of a potted Grevillea 'Little Red' among other nursery plants.](/assets/images/2024/img_1273.jpg)

*Apparently I needed to examine the back of the 'Little Red' label as well. These are serious decisions when you're planning an imaginary garden!*
{: .media-caption }

I mean it doesn't really but here they are.

![Grevillea 'LegacyFlame' plant label showing orange-red flower clusters beside green potted foliage on a nursery shelf.](/assets/images/2024/img_1274.jpg)

*Then I spotted 'LegacyFlame', with a rather impressive picture of red flowers. If only choosing a garden were as easy as collecting plant labels.*
{: .media-caption }

So many types, I'd definitely want a prostrate type - meaning ground cover.

![Hand holding the back of a Grevillea 'LegacyFlame' plant label describing its care and warning that it may irritate skin.](/assets/images/2024/img_1275.jpg)

*Aha, the back of the 'LegacyFlame' label even warns that it may be a skin irritant. Maybe not the ideal plant to tumble into, then.*
{: .media-caption }

But most of them are spiky...

![Nursery display packed with potted grevillea shrubs beneath a large 'Grevilleas' sign, with shelves of terracotta pots behind.](/assets/images/2024/img_1276.jpg)

*So many grevilleas in one place! I'd want a mixture of heights and shapes, rather than just a garden full of prickly surprises.*
{: .media-caption }

I would not want to fall into a grevillea bush.

![Angled view along a broad nursery grevillea display with numerous potted shrubs, other tall native plants, and terracotta pots in the background.](/assets/images/2024/img_1277.jpg)

*And the grevillea section just kept going. I could already imagine filling the entire place with plants, assuming the landlord didn't mind!*
{: .media-caption }

Or even a stand of grevillea! I guess I'd also like bottlebrush-type trees. Maybe some [Viola hederacea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola_hederacea) or similar in the shaded areas.

![Low spreading green shrub with a few pink flower spikes in a red-gravel garden bed against a brick wall, beside grass.](/assets/images/2024/img_1278.jpg)

*The actual garden is full of low shrubs, but this definitely isn't the weeping grass I'd choose instead of a regular lawn!*
{: .media-caption }

Weeping grass, [Microlaena stipoides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microlaena_stipoides), would be high up on my list for a lawn replacement. The above is definitely not weeping grass.

![Close-up of green lance-shaped leaves with two upright reddish flower spikes emerging from a dense ground-cover shrub.](/assets/images/2024/img_1279.jpg)

*A closer look at the ground cover already growing here. I quite like the dense greenery, even if I'm still daydreaming about other plants.*
{: .media-caption }

Creeping Boobialla, [Myoporum parvifolium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myoporum_parvifolium) is hardy so might make the list if the grevillea doesn't outcompete it.

![Wide garden bed of low green shrubs over reddish gravel along a brick house wall, bordered by grass and timber edging.](/assets/images/2024/img_1280.jpg)

*The garden outside our brick wall isn't bad, really. I just wish I could be sure we'd get another year here to enjoy it.*
{: .media-caption }

I guess it's all just a pipe dream though. Who knows if we'll get another year on the lease. The uncertainty of housing is one of the most depressing things I get to think about.

![Leafy carrot-like plants growing in dark mulch inside a green planter beside a brick wall, with a bright blue hose looping over the rim.](/assets/images/2024/img_1281.jpg)

*My idea of a lizard lounge: a planter full of greenery, with a handy blue hose for decor. The resident bar-sided skink was playing hide-and-seek in this shot!*
{: .media-caption }

Maybe I'll just live in some broken ceramic pots - a potential lizard lounge maybe? This little lizard, a [bar-sided skink (Concinnia tenuis)](https://inaturalist.ala.org.au/taxa/538970-Concinnia-tenuis), has no trouble hunting bugs in the carrot planter! 🥰
