Oh yes, I remember Yamaha's VQF quite well. Back in the late 1990s, when MP3 was taking over the internet, Yamaha had another audio format that looked like it might give MP3 a proper run for its money. Smaller files, supposedly better sound quality and less time waiting for music to download over a dial-up connection. What wasn't there to like?

VQF was based on TwinVQ, an audio compression technology developed by Japan's NTT and marketed by Yamaha under the SoundVQ name. The big selling point was that a 96 kbps stereo VQF file could supposedly compete with MP3 files encoded at considerably higher bitrates. Some people even reckoned it sounded better! Considering how much hard drive space cost back then, and how painfully slow downloading anything could be, this sounded like a game changer.

Unfortunately, there was one rather important problem. Encoding the bloody things took forever! You'd think an AMD Athlon 500 MHz processor would be up to the task, but apparently not. Here was a format promising to save precious megabytes, provided you were willing to sacrifice a ridiculous amount of time actually creating the files. Oh, a 500 MHz processor just doesn't do the job sometimes!

And then there was the software. Yamaha had its SoundVQ encoder and player, and there were even plugins for Winamp, but MP3 already had a massive head start. Everybody had MP3 files, everybody knew what an MP3 was, and just about every music player supported them. VQF might have had some technical advantages, but convincing everybody to change formats was another matter entirely.

These days I'm into CBR 320 kbps or VBR V0. I didn't used to be, mind you. There was a time when I didn't believe in V0 or V2 at all. Never thought I'd come around to variable bitrate encoding, but here we are. Still, what's V0 compared with a steaming pile of 96 kbps stereo music that takes a year to encode compared with MP3?

It's a shame really, because VQF was an interesting little piece of audio compression history. At a time when every megabyte mattered, the prospect of getting decent sound quality into a much smaller file was genuinely exciting. Unfortunately for Yamaha, being technically clever wasn't enough. MP3 was already everywhere, computers weren't exactly overflowing with processing power, and VQF never quite caught on.

I do wonder how things might have turned out if encoding had been faster and the format had enjoyed wider support. Perhaps we'd all have folders full of VQF files instead of MP3s. Then again, maybe we'd still be waiting for the first album to finish encoding! :)
