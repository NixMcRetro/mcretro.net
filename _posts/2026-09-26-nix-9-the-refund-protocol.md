*A true consumer dispute, retold as a cyberpunk quest. Featuring one terrible hotel room, a cancelled flight, several corporate scribes, and the most dangerous weapon in the known universe: arithmetic.*

## Prologue: A Thousand Credits

In the rain-soaked megacity, beneath towers bearing the logos of corporations nobody remembered electing, lived a netrunner known as Nix-9. She travelled with a trusted companion, carried a fixed stipend, and knew enough of the Ancient Statutes of Fair Trade to be dangerous to an unhelpful customer service department.

The pair had paid the Stellar Consortium of Orbital Journeys 1,000 credits for a package: accommodation in the Southern Sector and return passage aboard a star-liner. It should have been straightforward. Of course, in a dystopia, *straightforward* is usually where the trouble begins.

## I. The Cracked Chamber

![Nix-9 discovers the shabby, leaking Nexus Spire hotel chamber](/assets/images/2026/09/nix-9-the-refund-protocol/chapter-1.jxl)

The Nexus Spire Habitat looked acceptable from a distance. Inside, the air-conditioning was ancient and largely beyond the control of its occupants. Worse, a suspicious mouldy smell hung around the laundry equipment and refrigerator. A delightful little sanctuary, provided your idea of relaxation involved wondering what was growing behind the appliances.

Nix-9 complained. The Habitat Wardens offered a restrictive voucher, which was rather less useful than compensation in actual credits. She went back to the Consortium, the company that had sold the package, and pressed for a proper remedy.

Eventually, the Wardens agreed to refund 100 credits. The accommodation portion of the booking was worth 500, making the refund 20 per cent of that component. The Consortium confirmed it would be returned to her original payment method. One battle won. Unfortunately, the return journey was about to become considerably more interesting.

## II. The Cancelled Star-Liner

![The travellers face a cancelled star-liner in a neon spaceport](/assets/images/2026/09/nix-9-the-refund-protocol/chapter-2.jxl)

On departure day, the first neural-link notification announced a delay. Then came the cancellation. Orbital-lane restrictions, apparently. Nix-9 and her companion were now stranded, and the Consortium's promised updates became a small masterpiece of corporate indecision.

Another update in twenty minutes. No, forty. Or perhaps they should contact the Consortium themselves if nothing arrived in fifteen. The messages kept coming; useful assistance was harder to locate.

Being left to sort out a disrupted journey was no small inconvenience. Nix-9 kept the alerts, with their timestamps, and arranged another way home rather than entrusting the rest of the evening to the great corporate oracle of *please stand by*. She reached home. Her complaint, however, had barely left the launch pad.

## III. The Dungeon of Customer Service

![Nix-9 works through endless corporate correspondence and receipts](/assets/images/2026/09/nix-9-the-refund-protocol/chapter-3.jxl)

The airline acknowledged the disruption and distress but declined compensation. It said she had ultimately reached her destination and directed her to the Consortium, which had sold the package. The Consortium, in turn, had an impressive array of scribes and an apparently endless supply of polite ways to say no.

An early proposal was another 100-credit voucher for a future stay. This was not especially tempting, given that Nix-9 had just spent an uncomfortable amount of time escaping the consequences of the previous one. She declined.

The Consortium asked for the messages that had caused her distress. Fortunately, Nix-9 had saved them all: the delay, the cancellation, and the shifting promises about when somebody might actually help. The complete little archive went back across the net.

The Consortium maintained that notification had been prompt, that alternatives had been available, and that the disruption had been handled appropriately. Nix-9 disagreed, using the timestamps and the sequence of events rather than a spectacular outburst to make her case. She wasn't demanding that the Consortium control the weather or the orbital lanes. She wanted it to take responsibility for how the disruption had been handled.

Eventually, another offer appeared: 100 credits as a goodwill refund. Better than a voucher, certainly. But Nix-9 wasn't finished.

## IV. The Mathematical Reckoning

![Nix-9 uses percentages and arithmetic to challenge a refund offer](/assets/images/2026/09/nix-9-the-refund-protocol/chapter-4.jxl)

The complete package had cost 1,000 credits. The accommodation component was 500 credits, as the Consortium would eventually confirm. That left 500 credits attributable to the journey.

A 100-credit offer against that flight component amounted to just 20 per cent. Nix-9 requested 200 credits instead: a 40 per cent partial refund, which she considered a more reasonable response to the disruption and its handling. Not a demand for everything back. Not an arbitrary number pulled from a glowing terminal. An explained figure, tied to the price of the affected service.

The Consortium responded by confirming the precise breakdown: 500 credits for accommodation and 500 for flights. It also repeated its 100-credit offer and pointed Nix-9 towards the formal escalation channel.

This was an unfortunate tactical decision. The corporate scribes had now supplied the very figures that made her argument easier to follow.

Nix-9 wrote again. She thanked them for confirming the numbers, explained why 100 credits fell short, and repeated the 200-credit request. If the matter could not be resolved, she said, she would pursue the relevant external complaint channels.

No threats of orbital bombardment. No elaborate hacking sequence. Just evidence, persistence, and percentages. Absolutely devastating.

## V. The Accord of Credits

![The travellers celebrate an agreed settlement at an old computer](/assets/images/2026/09/nix-9-the-refund-protocol/chapter-5.jxl)

At last, a new message arrived from the Senior Scribe. The Consortium was prepared to process the additional 200-credit refund to the original payment method. Nix-9 accepted, in a reply of breathtaking simplicity: yes please, proceed.

The 100-credit accommodation refund had already been processed. Together with the newly agreed 200 credits for the flight dispute, the settlement came to **300 credits**, or 30 per cent of the original package price.

There was only one final boss left: the Banking Houses. At the time this chronicle was written, the additional 200 credits had been agreed and accepted but were still awaiting payment. Victory had been secured on paper; the final transfer remained outstanding.

## Epilogue: The Netrunner's Field Notes

![Nix-9 reviews her carefully kept records overlooking the cyberpunk city](/assets/images/2026/09/nix-9-the-refund-protocol/epilogue.jxl)

If there is a lesson in this saga, it isn't that every cancelled journey deserves a particular refund. Remedies depend on the circumstances, and a company's original explanation may not be the final word. It is that a complaint becomes much harder to dismiss when the facts are clear and the request is specific.

Save the notifications. Keep track of what was promised and when. Separate the different problems instead of letting them blur together. Work out the numbers, propose a reasonable resolution, and keep the correspondence. If you have to escalate, you will want a trail that another human can actually follow.

The corporations still loom over the neon skyline. The forms are still interminable. The hold music is still dreadful. But somewhere in the Southern Sector, a netrunner has secured agreement for 300 credits in refunds and proven that the ancient art of keeping receipts remains remarkably effective.
